I attended a Conducting Masterclass on September 3, 2026, at the Centro Escolar University (CEU) Conservatory of Music, led by Timothy Pallett. In the masterclass, we sang [[#Shosholoza]], and we learned a dance called the [[#Madiba Shuffle]]. I took some notes and went into a bit of research. I hope you find this insightful and thank you for taking the time to read.

Timothy Pallett is a conductor and choral clinician from Pretoria, South Africa. He presented at the International Federation for Choral Music (IFCM) Online Café, and spoke at the World Symposium on Choral Music 2026 in Macau on "Choral Works by Emerging South African Composers."

## Shosholoza

Tim introduced us to *Shosholoza*, a South African folk song usually described as a miner's song sung with a mix of Ndebele and Zulu. It's often called South Africa's second national anthem.

The version we sang used these lyrics:

	Shosholoza
	Kulezo ntaba
	Stimela siphume South Africa
	Wen' uyabaleka
	Kulezo ntaba
	Stimela siphume South Africa

Rough Translation:

	Move forward
	On those mountains,
	The steam train to South Africa
	You are running away
	On those mountains
	The steam train to South Africa

Shosholoza is a work song believed to have originated in Zimbabwe (formerly Rhodesia) brought by Rhodesian migrants who worked in the South African gold and diamond mines and traveled back and forth between Zimbabwe and South Africa.

The word "shosholoza" is a Ndebele word that means "go forward." It's also onomatopoeic, the *sho-sho* mimicking the sound of the train.

It was sung as a call-and-response, one voice leading and the group answering. That's how it kept the work in time. Men swinging shovels and axes needed a rhythm to move together, and the song gave them one. It also gave them something else—company, morale, a way to make a long shift feel shorter. Singing while working is one of the oldest things people do, and this song is a good example of why.

Nelson Mandela sang it while imprisoned on Robben Island. He said the singing made the work lighter, and he described the song as comparing the apartheid struggle to the motion of an oncoming train. After apartheid ended, it became a song of reconciliation. South Africans of every background sing it now, most memorably in the film *Invictus*, where a stadium full of people sing it together to carry the Springboks through the 1995 Rugby World Cup.

![Invictus #9 Movie CLIP - This is Our Destiny (2009) HD](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KE-ok-meF3E)

So this is a song with several lives. It began as a work song, became a protest song, then a prison song, and now a national one. It carried all of that without changing much about itself.

## Madiba Shuffle

At some point during the masterclass, Tim taught us a dance. He called it the Madiba Shuffle.

Madiba is Nelson Mandela's clan name. The shuffle was his—his signature dance move. A double fist raised in a curved position, a slight shake of the body, the legs moving around the knees. Nothing flashy. He first did it publicly at his 72nd birthday celebration in 1990, his first birthday as a free man after twenty-seven years in prison. He danced to the music of Thandi Klaasen at Kippies in Johannesburg. He danced again in 1999, on stage with Johnny Clegg in France, and told the crowd, "It is music and dancing that makes me at peace with the world and at peace with myself."

Tim's instruction while teaching us was simple: **keep it small and groovy.**

That's the whole thing. Not big, not showy, not performative. Small, and groovy.

The point isn't the size of the movement. It's the ease. Mandela's shuffle wasn't remarkable because it was big or technically impressive. It was remarkable because it was relaxed, rhythmic, and completely at home in itself. Anyone can do it. That's part of what made it his.

It's also worth noting what kind of dance it is—one that asks for restraint instead of display, and groove instead of spectacle. I think that's why Tim taught it in a conducting masterclass. It's a good thing to have in your body.

## Cueing

Tim said something about cueing that stuck with me:

> "The sheet music already knows what to do."

What I understood him to mean is that the information is already there. The notes, the dynamics, the entrances—they exist on the page before the conductor raises their hands. The conductor's job is not just to create the music. It's to disseminate it, interpret it, and cue the parts that need cuing.

That sounds obvious until you sit with it. The music does still depend on the conductor—the gesture starts the ensemble, shapes the phrase, and holds it all together. But there's a difference between guiding the music and supplying it. The temptation is to lean toward the latter: to conduct as though the sound won't happen without you, to cue things that were already going to happen, to fill silence with motion. It's an easy habit to fall into.

If the sheet music already knows what to do, then the conductor's job is not to be the source of the music. It's to be a facilitator of it.

That reframing sits underneath everything else in this masterclass.

## Just Be Cool

The three most important words, according to Tim: **"Just be cool."**

During the Q&A, I asked him what he meant. Was it something he learned from the culture of South African music? Did it have to do with the communal side of South African culture?

His answer, as I recorded it: for South African pieces, according to his experience, you have to just be cool.

I don't think he meant detachment. I don't think he meant don't feel anything. I think he meant something closer to ease—trust, flow, the refusal to force. The music already knows what to do. The ensemble already knows what to do. The conductor's anxiety doesn't help either of them. It only gets in the way.

Cool here isn't distance. It's composure. It's the calm of someone who has done the work and can now let the music move through them without grabbing at it.

## Ubuntu

Underneath "just be cool" is something older and larger.

Ubuntu is an Nguni Bantu term meaning _humanity_, usually translated as "I am because we are" or "humanity towards others." It comes from the Zulu proverb _Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu_—a person is a person through other people. The idea appears across the Bantu language family, as _unhu_ in Shona, _Obuntu_ in Uganda and Tanzania, and other variants throughout sub-Saharan Africa.

Desmond Tutu, a South African bishop and theologian, explained it this way:

> "None of us comes into the world fully formed. We would not know how to think, or walk, or speak, or behave as human beings unless we learned it from other human beings. We need other human beings in order to be human. I am because other people are."

A [2025 paper on Ubuntu in social work](https://doi.org/10.1177/14680173241312749) identifies its core values as relationality, collective responsibility, communal accountability, social justice, recognition, and reciprocity. These aren't abstract ideals. They're practical principles for how people live and work together. I'll focus on a few of them here, since they're the ones that connect most directly to what I watched.

**Relationality** means that all things exist in relatedness. It's hard to imagine a person existing outside that network. Applied to conducting, this isn't a small idea. If I am because we are, then the ensemble isn't simply the conductor's instrument. The sound the choir makes is co-created, and the conductor's part in that is smaller and more delicate than the podium usually suggests.

**Collective responsibility** means our humanity is mutually constitutive. As Tutu also put it, "I am human because I belong, I participate, I share." The conductor's authority is real. Someone has to be responsible for the whole. But Ubuntu suggests that authority is exercised within the ensemble, not above it, and that it exists to serve the music and the people making it.

**Recognition** and **reciprocity** go together. The greeting _salibonani_ in Zimbabwe means "we have seen each other" or "I can see you." Recognition is acknowledging someone before anything else begins. For a conductor, that means seeing the ensemble first—as people, not just as sound-producers—before asking them to make music.

Tim's casual friendliness wasn't just personality. It was Ubuntu in practice. He didn't stand apart from us as an authority. He was with us, talking with us, laughing with us. That's what relational leadership looks like when it isn't being theorized. It's just being done.

## Beginner and Experienced

My second question was this: beyond cueing, what actually separates a beginner conductor from an experienced one?

His answer: the comfort and confidence of the conductor.

I think that's right, and I think it's simpler than it sounds. An experienced conductor has done it more. They've stood in front of more ensembles, worked through more difficult passages, and made more mistakes in front of people. That's where the comfort comes from. Nothing mysterious about it.

The encouraging part is that a beginner can get there too. It just takes the work. Study your repertoire intently. Familiarize yourself with the piece—the language, the historical context, the performance practice, the spots where the ensemble usually struggles. Do it enough times and the confidence starts to arrive on its own.

Both comfort and confidence are forms of familiarity. And familiarity is built by practice.

So the answer folds back into the earlier idea. The sheet music already knows what to do—but you have to know the sheet music. The conductor's calm isn't innate. It's the visible surface of unseen preparation.
