I had the chance to attend Michelle Mariposa's Voice Masterclass at the UP College of Music on September 2, 2026. I'm not a singer myself, but there were plenty of things I picked up from her lecture. These are my notes, and I hope they'll be useful to singers and other musicians alike. 

Michelle Mariposa is a Filipino-Chinese mezzo-soprano who made history as one of the Grand Finals winners of the prestigious 2025 Metropolitan Opera Eric and Dominique Laffont Competition, where she received the Faith P. Geier Award.

---
## On Memorization 

> *"Memorization is not the goal, mastery is."*

This really stuck with me. It's not enough to simply memorize the content of the piece. You need to research it—be historically informed, understand the meaning behind the words, know the context and background of the piece, and more.

Her tips for memorizing:

- Listen to the whole piece and mark the areas you feel will be a problem.
- Work on your keyboard skills. It helps to be able to sing along with the harmony, even at least the bass line.
- Practice your solfege.
- There's no quick hack. Just simple repetition, refinement, and hard work.
- Work backwards. She likes to work from the sections at the end of the piece to the beginning.
- Create vocal exercises from the difficult areas.

She also mentioned that her gold standard is to memorize all the parts—even the other performers' parts—by the beginning of the rehearsal process. This really aligns with her goal of mastery.

It makes sense. Listening to the whole piece gives you perspective. Listening to different recordings and performances adds to your interpretive arsenal. It's simply a responsibility to be aware and informed about the music you'll be performing.

On keyboard skills, I agree—and it's not just about playing the melody and singing along. Being able to sing with the harmony, even just the bass line, deepens your understanding of the relationship between the notes.

I also find it true that there's no "hack" in the sense of something that will suddenly solve the problem or make memorization easy overnight. There are really just smarter and more efficient ways of approaching the work.

For example, chunking—breaking the piece into smaller sections—is one way of working more intelligently. You can practice those sections individually and gradually refine them, rather than always running through the entire piece from beginning to end.

Working backwards is another approach. By starting with the sections at the end and gradually adding the sections that come before them, you become less dependent on the preceding material as a cue for what comes next.

I also really like her suggestion of turning difficult passages into vocal exercises. Instead of repeating a difficult section over and over and hoping it eventually becomes easier, you identify what specifically makes it difficult and isolate that problem. Then you create an exercise that targets it directly. The goal isn't to find a shortcut around the work, but to make the practice itself more purposeful.

## On Nerves and Performance

In the lecture, she was asked how to handle being nervous before a performance. 

- It's better to be nervous. It helps with focusing on your performance, referring to it as a "performance high."

I like that she referred to it as a "performance high." It's basically being in the zone or being in the "flow state." Nervousness is not something necessarily negative or something that needs to be eliminated before performing. A certain level of nervousness can increase your alertness and concentration. I understand this feeling and it's quite exhilarating. Of course I had times of absolute anxiety before a performance, but I also had times I had a mix of confidence and nervousness which certainly best describes that "performance high."

## On Melismas

For the general approach:

- Memorize the principal notes first. Learn the main melodic line before adding the ornaments.

Practicing Melismas:

- Group the notes. When there are many notes in a melisma, break them into smaller groups so you don't get lost.
- Count the notes. Know where each note falls within the group rather than singing through the melisma blindly.

>[!info]- What's a Melisma?
>A string of multiple notes sung in one syllable. 

Her tip on grouping notes connects back to the chunking I mentioned earlier. For counting, she sang the melodic line while counting the beats aloud, like "1-2-3-4, 1-2-3-4," following the rhythm. This creates a mental marker for where each note falls.

While this was a voice masterclass, her tips can be applied to practice in general. They just take different forms depending on the instrument.
