## Piano Technology: The Basics

### Piano Technology is Like Music Theory

Just as music theory gives us a framework for understanding notes and harmony, piano technology has its own set of technical theories and concepts. The seminar introduced these fundamentals:

- **Sound Waves:** The visual measurement of sound and how it occurs.
- **Compound Tones and Overtones:** The characteristics of a single pitch.
- **Sympathetic Resonance:** An acoustic phenomenon that occurs in the strings.
- **Pitch, Intensity, Duration, Timbre:** The four elements of musical tone.

The seminar also introduced a simple formula: 

$$
Wavelength = \frac{340}{Frequency}
$$

Where 340 represents the speed of sound through air (340m/sec at 15°C).

### The Parts That Make the Sound

The piano is a system of interconnected parts, and each one has a specific role.

**Hammers and Strings**
The hammers and strings together determine the tone quality of the piano.
- **Hammers:** The felt on the hammer can be adjusted through a process called **voicing**. Soft hammers produce a mellow sound, while hard hammers produce a bright sound.
- **Strings:** The material and build quality of the strings determine tone quality and create the pitch. A standard piano has **230 strings**, and they exert a combined total of **20 tons of tension** on the piano. The strings must also be stiff enough to hold tension but flexible enough to vibrate.

**The Bridge and Soundboard**
These parts amplify the vibration of the strings.
- **The Bridge:** Carries the vibrations of the strings to the soundboard. It is adhered to the soundboard.
- **The Soundboard:** The "heart" of the piano. It amplifies the sound of the strings. It must be durable and flexible enough to vibrate with the strings, and it is made of spruce. Importantly, the soundboard is **not flat** — it is curved, and this curve is called the **crown**.

### Sympathetic Resonance

This is one of the most beautiful concepts in piano technology. **Sympathetic resonance** is an acoustic phenomenon involving the **overtone series**. A single pitch actually contains many other pitches, called **overtones**. When two pitches share an overtone — a **common overtone** — they can vibrate in sympathy with each other. For example, G4 is a common overtone of C3.

Sympathetic resonance is most prominent on the piano because of how close together its strings are. In performance, it results in a more resonant sound, especially when the dampers are lifted (for instance, when the sustain pedal is pressed). This is part of what gives the piano its extraordinary depth and richness.

## What is the Piano? Upright vs. Grand

### Size and Tone Escapement

There are practical differences between upright and grand pianos that affect how they sound and how the pianist hears them.

- **Tone Escapement:** Sound from an upright piano naturally escapes from the back and the top (when the lid is open). Sound from a grand piano naturally escapes from the top and the bottom. A pianist can more easily hear the music when playing on a grand piano.
- **Size:** The tallest upright piano is 131cm (4.2 feet), such as the Yamaha YUS5. The shortest grand piano is 151cm (5 feet), such as the Yamaha GB1K.
- **Effect on Performance:** A bigger soundboard produces a louder sound. Longer strings improve the roundness of the bass and the clarity of the treble.

### Why String Length Matters

Piano strings need to meet three conditions to create a clear tone:
1. Strong enough to withstand a high amount of tension.
2. Flexible enough to vibrate.
3. Long enough to create the correct pitch.

Ideally, longer strings meet all these requirements, which is why larger pianos generally have a richer, fuller sound.

### The Piano Action: Upright vs. Grand

The action is the mechanism that transfers the pianist's touch to the strings. The fundamental difference between upright and grand actions explains why grands are preferred for advanced playing.

**Upright Piano Action:**
- Hammers move **left** to strike strings and **right** to return to rest. They cannot use gravity to return to rest.
- Repetition rate of **7 notes per second** (does not have a repetition lever).
- Produces a noticeable gap in its touch when playing trills or repeated notes.

**Grand Piano Action:**
- Hammers move **up** to strike the strings and **fall** to return to rest. They use gravity to return to rest.
- Repetition rate of **14 notes per second** with the aid of a repetition lever.
- Little to no gap at all in its touch when playing trills or repeated notes.

## About Tuning

### Tuning Overview

Piano tuning is tricky for several reasons. A technician needs to tune 230 strings, requires specialized tools (a tuning hammer and mutes), and cannot rely on piano tuning apps. The work requires accuracy and stability.

Common tuning issues that owners experience include:
- The piano still sounds untuned after being tuned.
- The piano quickly becomes out of tune.
- The piano sounds "off" despite being tuned.

**The Tools of the Trade**

Joshua showed the specialized tools a technician uses: a tuning hammer and various mutes (Long Mute in 3.0mm, 4.5mm, and 5.0mm Red, 4mm Green, Rubber Mute, and a Wood Stick Mute).

### Common Explanations for Tuning Issues

When a piano has tuning problems, the cause can usually be traced to one of three sources.

**Fault of the Piano**

- The piano is too old.
- Pin block holes have expanded.
- The piano can't maintain high tension from the strings.

**Lack of Maintenance**

- The piano is left untuned for too long (over 4-5 years).
- The soundboard cannot take the sudden high tension.
- The strings will resist the new tuning frequency (440 Hz).

**Fault of the Tuner**

- The tuner is not skilled enough or lacks practice.
- A weak ear leads to overreliance on the tuning app.
- The tuner is unaware of or misunderstands stability.
- Improper tuning technique.

### The Effects of Tuning: Theory and Performance

**In Theory:**
Untuned pianos will have less volume, produce an audible pulse called a **beat**, and generally sound cheap or low quality. A beat occurs when two tones of slightly different frequencies are played together — the sound becomes louder and softer in regular periodic cycles, and the number of beats per second equals the difference between the frequencies.

**In Practice and Performance:**
Untuned pianos have a real impact on the musician:
- They impact a pianist's ability to project their sound.
- They make voicing, phrasing, and shaping more difficult.
- They impact the general clarity of the main melodic line.
- They facilitate improper listening practice.

## Maintenance

### The Real Impact of Proper Maintenance

The seminar addressed some common statements about piano care: "A piano must be tuned at least once a year," "Humidity is very bad for your piano," and "Don't put your piano against the window."

These aren't just old wives' tales. Prolonged loss of tension will affect the **crown of the soundboard**. And humidity and temperature changes can cause the wood and felt parts to swell or shrink.

### Five Simple Steps for Maintaining Your Piano

Based on this [article](https://hub.yamaha.com/pianos/p-how-to/five-simple-steps-for-maintaining-your-piano/) written by Jerry Kovarsky on Yamaha's official maintenance guidance, here are five practical steps every piano owner can take.

**1. Keep It In Tune**

Regular tuning keeps your piano sounding its best. If you let it go too long, it can be harder (and more expensive) to bring back to perfect condition. As a rule of thumb, have your piano tuned **at least once a year**, though instruments that are played often or are brand new should be tuned twice a year or more. Do not attempt tuning yourself — this can cause damage. Find a professionally trained tuner and stick with them so they get to know your instrument and your home.

**2. Adjust the Pedals**

Always ask your technician to check the pedals during a tuning visit. Grand and baby grand pianos have three pedals; uprights typically have two. If pedals are not working optimally, there may be too much "play," meaning they won't respond as quickly as they should. If your piano has a squeaky pedal, do not spray lubricant into it yourself — have your technician investigate the actual location of the problem.

**3. Regulate the Action As Necessary**

An acoustic piano has upwards of **10,000 moving parts** that need to be set to exacting specifications. Two critical settings are **let-off** (the point at which the hammer mechanism slips free of the key) and **aftertouch** (the small distance the key still travels downward afterward). Too little aftertouch and the keyboard action will feel hard and bottom out abruptly. Too much aftertouch and the action will feel mushy, and the keys will be slow to respond when releasing notes. Have your technician check these settings **during every other tuning**. This work is called **regulation**.

**4. Control the Humidity**

Humidity — not heat or cold — is the real enemy of the piano, and is the likely cause of stuck or squeaky keys. Yamaha recommends a humidity range of **35-55%, with 45% being optimal**. Avoid placing the piano near direct vents or radiators. Use an HVAC system with a humidistat control, or buy a simple digital thermometer with a humidity reading and place it near the piano. If necessary, use a small room dehumidifier or humidifier placed near (but not too close to) the piano.

**5. Keep It Clean**

Keep the lid of your grand piano **down when not being played** to protect the insides from dust and dirt. Keep the key cover closed when not in use. To dust the case, use a simple cotton cloth or T-shirt. **Do not use microfiber rags** — they tend to hold onto dust and push it around, and can actually charge the surface to attract more dust. Avoid furniture polish; for fingerprints or smudges, use a small amount of window cleaner sprayed onto the cloth. To clean the keys, use a diluted solution of dishwashing liquid and water on a soft rag, followed by a clean, slightly damp one. **Do not use any alcohol-based cleaner** — it can cause the keys to discolor and/or crack over time.

## Who Are the Piano Technicians?

### Piano Technician Levels

The seminar defined three levels of piano technicians:

- **Tuner:** Handles only tuning.
- **Technician:** Can handle tuning, action regulation, and repair.
- **Restoration Specialist:** On top of tuning and regulation, they can completely tear down the piano and restore old pianos.

Tuning is a technician's "bread and butter" because, like any instrument, pianos will always need tuning. **Regulation** requires more skill and knowledge — if executed properly, the piano's touch can be returned to a playable state (results may vary). **Restoration** is the highest level, requires a proper workshop, and can take up to **1 year to fully restore 1 piano**.

### The Philippine Instrument Technician Situation

This was one of the most eye-opening parts of the seminar. In the Philippines, instrument technicians and musicians suffer from the same lack of support and appreciation.

- Most technicians are self-taught or receive training from a family member (family tradition).
- Most clients can't pay past a certain amount for tuning, regardless of quality.
- There are only **7 officially trained piano technicians in the Philippines** (as of this writing).

**Opportunities Abroad**
- In Japan, Yamaha and Kawai provide training (for a price) with a system for employment after their training.
- The **Piano Technicians Guild** is a US-based organization for piano service and technology. It is the largest non-profit organization serving piano tuners and technicians throughout the world, with over 3,300 global members.

### Paths to Certification

For anyone interested in pursuing this as a career — and for myself, since I hope to get certified someday — the seminar pointed to two major paths:

- **Yamaha Piano Technical Academy:** Located next to the Yamaha piano factory in Kakegawa, Japan, the academy was established in 1980 and has trained more than 2,000 piano technicians to date. It offers a "General Course" starting from beginner level, an "Advanced Course," a "GP Course" and "GP Master Course" for grand pianos, and a "Concert Piano Course" representing the pinnacle of piano tuning and technical training.

- **Piano Technicians Guild (PTG):** The PTG grants the **Registered Piano Technician (RPT)** credential after a series of rigorous examinations testing skill in piano tuning, regulation, and repair. To attain the RPT accreditation, a PTG member must pass three examinations. Though the RPT designation is the only accreditation in the field currently available in North America.

## A Closing Word on Why This Matters

So why does any of this matter – tuning, humidity, regulation, all these parts and processes?

Because a piano goes out of tune over time whether or not anyone plays it. Temperature and humidity shift, strings move, and the instrument drifts away from where it should be. It happens quietly, which is exactly what makes it easy to ignore.

A regularly tuned piano:

- Sounds clearer, more resonant, and more enjoyable to play
- Makes practice and rehearsals more productive
- Helps preserve the instrument and prevents long-term issues
- Allows musicians to develop a more accurate sense of pitch

An out-of-tune piano can quietly affect musical learning. When students practice or rehearse with an instrument that isn't properly tuned, it can interfere with pitch recognition and intonation. This is especially important for singers, who rely on a stable, accurate pitch to train their ears and voices.

Whether your piano is used for practice, teaching, accompanying, or performance, keeping it in tune supports better musicianship and a better learning environment. That's the whole point — not perfection, just an instrument that lets you do your best work.
